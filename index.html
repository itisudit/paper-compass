About the practice

Why Paper
Compass?

Paper Compass is built around a simple idea: a primary research paper should not be read like a novel. The goal is to move from passive reading to active analysis — deconstruct the authors’ arguments, examine the evidence, and form your own conclusion.

Find → orient → select → interrogate → judge → remember

Broad reading and deep reading are different jobs

Broad reading helps you navigate a large literature quickly. Search, screen, compare, and orient yourself across a field before deciding which papers deserve your scarce attention.

Deep reading is for the papers that could materially shape your research question. It is deliberately slower and more demanding: establish context, test the methods, inspect the evidence, reach your own conclusion, and only then compare it with the authors’ interpretation.

The article behind this project explicitly describes the deep-dive method as an intensive process intended for high-impact papers rather than broad background reading, and the author says he avoids AI for the deep-dive so that the reader retains their own critical judgement and scientific intuition.

The seven-step framework

Phase 1 · The aerial view
01 · Get a broad overview
02 · Identify the core research question
03 · Map the existing knowledge gap

Phase 2 · The interrogation
04 · Assess the methodology
05 · Reach your own conclusion from the results and figures

Phase 3 · The verdict
06 · Reconcile the authors’ conclusion against your own
07 · Consider alternative explanations or confounding factors

What Paper Compass adds

The framework is deliberately manual at the point where judgement matters. Paper Compass gives you a structured place to record your independent reading, lock your Step 5 verdict before opening the discussion, attach concerns to specific evidence, assess claims, and preserve the reasoning behind your conclusion.

The source

Jacques Cornwell, How to read a research paper, Nature 654, 828–829 (18 June 2026).

Phase 1 · Aerial view

Gather context before you get lost in details.

Keep in mind: don't let the introduction's framing become your framing. Note what's asserted vs. what's cited.
01

Get a broad overview

Scan the abstract, figures and legends. What kind of study is this, and what does its overall design try to establish?

  • If you removed the title, would the abstract alone tell you what kind of study this is?
  • Do the figures tell a coherent story on their own, without reading the text?
  • What is the single sentence you'd use to describe this paper to a colleague in the elevator?
02

Name the core question

Find the defining question at the end of the introduction. State the hypothesis precisely enough to check every later claim against it.

  • Is the question falsifiable — could an experiment plausibly return "no"?
  • Does the question match what the methods actually test, or is it broader/narrower?
  • What would a "yes" answer let you predict that you couldn't before?
03

Map the knowledge gap

What is already known? What missing piece does this work address? Why would closing that gap matter?

  • Is the "gap" genuinely unaddressed, or just unaddressed by this specific subfield's usual methods?
  • Who benefits if this gap is closed — theory, a clinical population, an industry?
  • Could the gap be explained by lack of interest rather than lack of feasibility?

Phase 2 · Interrogation

Evaluate whether the evidence can answer the question.

Keep in mind: methods and results deserve more of your time than the discussion. Read them slower than feels natural.
04

Appraise methods & power

Are the models, sampling, controls and analysis appropriate? Look for a power analysis, accessible raw data, and reproducible code or pipelines.

  • Is the sample size justified in advance, or only defensible in hindsight?
  • What would the ideal control group look like, and how far is the actual control from that?
  • Could the statistical test have been chosen because it gave a significant result?
  • If you tried to rerun this from the methods section alone, where would you get stuck?
05

Reach your conclusion first

Skip the discussion. Read results, graphs, tables and error bars. What do the data actually support—before you read the authors' interpretation?

  • Do the error bars/CIs overlap between the groups the text says differ?
  • Is the effect size meaningful, or just statistically significant?
  • Does every panel in the key figure support the headline claim, or only one of them?
  • What is the simplest, most boring explanation for this result?

    Phase 3 · Verdict

    Reconcile claims with evidence and name the boundaries.

    Keep in mind: distinguish what the authors overclaim from what they honestly flag as a limitation.
    06

    Reconcile conclusions

    Now read the discussion. Where do your conclusion and the authors' interpretation agree, diverge, or differ in confidence?

    • Do the authors' claims track the data, or do they extend beyond it into speculation?
    • Where the language hedges ("suggests", "may"), does your own reading agree with the hedge?
    • Is there a claim in the abstract that isn't fully backed by the results you saw?
    07

    Find confounders & limits

    Play devil's advocate. List unaddressed variables, acknowledged limitations, funding, and conflicts that should affect how strongly you trust the claims.

    • Who funded this, and could that funder benefit from a particular result?
    • What variable wasn't controlled that plausibly explains part of the effect?
    • Does the paper generalize past its actual population/model, in the abstract or press release?
    • If this were replicated with 10x the sample, what result would you actually expect?
    Manual practice, not AI substitution.
    Use this for high-impact papers; preserve your own scientific judgement.
